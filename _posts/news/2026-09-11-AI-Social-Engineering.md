## Introduction

Artificial intelligence is no longer confined to laboratories or specialist software. It is now woven into search engines, social-media platforms, banking systems, public services, cybersecurity tools, advertising networks, and everyday communication. The United Nations’ 2024 E-Government Survey assessed digital-government development across all 193 UN Member States and reported continuing worldwide growth in online public services. [publicadministration.desa.un](https://publicadministration.desa.un.org/publications/un-e-government-survey-2024-0)

This transformation creates an important paradox. AI can help societies detect fraud, improve healthcare, expand accessibility, analyze climate data, and deliver public services. At the same time, it can make deception more convincing, surveillance more extensive, and political influence more personalized.

This is the central subject of **AI social engineering in the digital age**.

The phrase does not mean that artificial intelligence can literally read or control the human mind. A more accurate claim is that AI can increase the scale, speed, realism, and personalization of attempts to influence human attention, judgment, emotion, and behavior. When such systems operate without transparency or accountability, they can weaken individual autonomy and democratic decision-making.

## What is social engineering?

Social engineering is the manipulation of people into taking actions that benefit another person or organization. Those actions may include:

- Revealing passwords or personal information.
- Transferring money.
- Opening a malicious file.
- Trusting a false identity.
- Sharing misleading content.
- Supporting a political claim or campaign.
- Accepting an automated decision without questioning it.

Social engineering does not primarily attack a computer’s technical weakness. It attacks the human relationship with technology.

A criminal may not need to break into a bank’s database if they can persuade an employee to transfer money. A political campaign may not need to change every voter’s ideology if it can discourage selected groups from voting. A malicious actor may not need to forge an entire identity if a cloned voice is convincing enough for one urgent transaction.

AI strengthens these methods by enabling automated content generation, voice imitation, translation, audience segmentation, behavioral analysis, and rapid experimentation.

## AI as a social technology

AI is often described as a technical system, but its consequences are social. A model is trained on data, deployed through an institution, and used by people within a particular economic and political environment.

A simple machine-learning formulation is:

$$
\theta^\ast=\arg\min_{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(f_\theta(x_i),y_i)
$$

Here, a model $f_\theta$ learns parameters $\theta$ by minimizing a loss function $L$ over examples $x_i$ and desired outputs $y_i$. This mathematical objective may produce a highly accurate model, but accuracy alone does not answer important social questions:

- Is the data lawful and representative?
- Is the system fair?
- Can people challenge its decisions?
- Does it expose private information?
- Can it be misused for manipulation?
- Who is responsible when it causes harm?

This is why AI governance must consider social context, not only model performance. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework organizes this work through the functions **Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage**. [nvlpubs.nist](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/nist.ai.100-1.pdf)

## What benefits can AI bring?

AI can provide substantial benefits when it is designed and governed responsibly.

### Public services

AI can help governments process applications, identify service gaps, translate information, detect anomalies, and respond to citizens more efficiently. The UN reports that digital-government development has advanced globally, although countries still differ significantly in infrastructure, human capacity, and access. [un](https://www.un.org/en/desa/digital-transformation-accelerates-gaps-underscore-need-new-framework-advance-use)

### Healthcare

AI can support medical-image analysis, clinical research, disease surveillance, personalized treatment planning, and administrative work. It should assist qualified professionals rather than replace medical judgment in situations involving serious risk.

### Scientific research

Researchers use AI to analyze large datasets, search scientific literature, optimize experiments, simulate systems, and identify patterns that may be difficult to detect manually. In mathematics and scientific computing, AI can support symbolic computation, numerical approximation, optimization, and theorem-proving workflows.

### Accessibility

Speech recognition, real-time captioning, translation, text-to-speech tools, and visual assistance can make information more accessible to people with disabilities or language barriers.

### Cybersecurity

AI can help identify suspicious network behavior, classify malware, detect phishing, and prioritize security alerts. Yet attackers can also use AI, so defensive systems require continuous testing and human supervision.

### Environmental management

AI can analyze satellite images, model weather and climate patterns, optimize energy systems, and support disaster early-warning systems.

These benefits are real, but they are not automatic. A system can be efficient while still being invasive, discriminatory, insecure, or impossible to challenge.

## Why can AI become dangerous?

The risks of AI social engineering emerge when several capabilities combine:

$$
\text{Personal data}+\text{behavioral prediction}+\text{synthetic content}+\text{automated distribution}
$$

Personal data helps identify audiences. Behavioral prediction estimates what may attract or persuade them. Generative AI produces tailored content. Digital platforms distribute and test that content at scale.

The result is not necessarily mind control. It is a more powerful influence environment in which people may not know:

- Who created a message.
- Why they received it.
- What data was used to target them.
- Whether other people received different information.
- Whether an image, voice, or video is authentic.
- Whether apparent public agreement is genuine.

## Human psychology and manipulation

People use mental shortcuts because they cannot independently verify every claim. These shortcuts are normally useful, but they can be exploited.

Common triggers include:

### Authority

People are more likely to comply with someone who appears to be a manager, police officer, bank representative, government official, or technical expert.

### Urgency

A message that creates time pressure discourages careful reasoning

> “Transfer the money immediately.”
> “Your account will be closed today.”
> “Do not contact anyone else.”

### Familiarity

A familiar name, photograph, writing style, or cloned voice can create trust.

### Fear

Fear reduces reflection and increases the desire for immediate action.

### Social proof

People may interpret high numbers of likes, shares, comments, or apparently independent accounts as evidence that a claim is true.

### Repetition

Repeated exposure can make a claim feel familiar, even when familiarity is mistaken for evidence.

No person should assume complete immunity to propaganda. Recognizing vulnerability is not an admission of weakness; it is a realistic foundation for better decision-making.

## Propaganda in the age of AI

Propaganda is organized communication intended to shape attitudes or behavior for a political, ideological, commercial, or institutional purpose. It is not defined only by whether a statement is false. A message can use selective facts, emotional framing, misleading omissions, repetition, or manufactured consensus.

Traditional propaganda relied on newspapers, posters, radio, television, speeches, and public events. AI-enabled propaganda adds:

- Automated generation of text and images.
- Personalized political messaging.
- Rapid translation into many languages.
- Synthetic voices and videos.
- Fake accounts and coordinated amplification.
- Continuous testing of message variations.
- Targeting based on behavioral data.

The basic process can be represented as:

$$
\text{Data collection}
\rightarrow
\text{Profiling}
\rightarrow
\text{Targeting}
\rightarrow
\text{Content generation}
\rightarrow
\text{Distribution}
\rightarrow
\text{Feedback}
$$

A campaign can observe which messages attract attention, identify the audiences most responsive to them, and generate new versions. This makes propaganda more adaptive than traditional one-message mass communication.

Nevertheless, personalized propaganda does not guarantee persuasion. People have prior beliefs, social networks, education, experience, and access to competing sources. The responsible claim is that AI increases the capacity for influence—not that it inevitably controls people.

## Social media and AI amplification

Social-media platforms do not simply display information in chronological order. Recommender systems rank, filter, and select content according to platform objectives and predictions about user behavior.

A simplified loop is:

1. The user interacts with content.
2. The platform records behavioral signals.
3. The system predicts what the user may engage with.
4. Similar content is recommended.
5. The user’s new behavior becomes additional data.

This loop can be beneficial when it helps users discover relevant educational material. It can be harmful when engagement incentives favor outrage, fear, polarization, or sensationalism.

AI can intensify this process by generating many variants of a message and tailoring them to different communities. UNESCO’s digital-platform guidelines emphasize transparency, accountability, human-rights protection, and safeguards against the amplification of harmful content through curation and recommender systems. [unesco](https://www.unesco.org/en/internet-trust/guidelines)

## Why objective reality matters for democracy

Democracy does not require citizens to agree about every value or policy. People may reasonably disagree about taxes, healthcare, education, or foreign policy.

However, democratic disagreement requires some shared and verifiable facts. Citizens need to establish, as reliably as possible:

- Whether an event occurred.
- Whether a document is authentic.
- Whether a public official made a statement.
- Whether an election result was counted correctly.
- Whether a government statistic is supported by evidence.

Without a shared factual foundation, public debate becomes difficult. Citizens may stop arguing about solutions and instead argue about whether reality itself exists.

This is why synthetic media creates a double danger. It can make false content appear authentic, but it can also allow real evidence to be dismissed as fake. If citizens conclude that every video, recording, or document may be fabricated, accountability becomes weaker.

## Deepfakes and voice-cloning fraud

A deepfake is synthetic or manipulated media that imitates a person or event. It may involve a face, voice, image, video, or combination of media.

Potential uses include:

- Fraudulent payment requests.
- Fake calls from relatives.
- Impersonation of business executives.
- Fabricated political statements.
- Non-consensual intimate imagery.
- Fake evidence in legal or personal disputes.
- False claims that authentic evidence is artificial.

The technical sophistication of a deepfake is only part of the problem. Social context makes it persuasive. A voice does not need to be perfect if the listener is distracted, worried, or under pressure.

For high-stakes requests, the correct rule is:

$$
\text{Trust in a voice} \neq \text{proof of identity}
$$

A person should verify the request through an independent communication channel, follow established payment procedures, and require additional approval for unusual transactions. NIST’s work on synthetic content discusses provenance, labeling, watermarking, detection, authentication, testing, and auditing as possible mitigation approaches, while recognizing that no single technique is sufficient. [nist](https://www.nist.gov/publications/reducing-risks-posed-synthetic-content-overview-technical-approaches-digital-content)

## Behavioral profiling and microtargeting

Digital platforms can collect direct information such as location, device type, browsing behavior, and interactions. They can also infer interests, personality traits, purchasing intentions, political preferences, and possible vulnerabilities.

**Microtargeting** involves delivering different messages to narrowly defined groups or individuals. It can be useful for relevant advertising or public-health communication, but it can also become politically troubling when citizens receive different factual claims without knowing that others are seeing different messages.

A responsible assessment asks:

- What information was collected?
- What characteristics were inferred?
- Was the person informed?
- Could the inference be wrong?
- Was sensitive information used?
- Could the person refuse the targeting?
- Was the message political or commercial?
- Could the person see who funded it?

The problem is not personalization itself. The problem is hidden personalization combined with deception, exploitation, and lack of accountability.

## Nudge theory and behavioral insight

A nudge changes the way choices are presented without formally eliminating alternatives. A reminder to renew a document, a clearer health message, or a privacy-protective default may help people make decisions that support their own interests.

Behavioral-insight teams study how people actually make decisions and test whether small changes improve public outcomes. Their work may involve psychology, economics, experiments, and careful measurement.

The ethical boundary depends on transparency and autonomy. A nudge is more defensible when it is:

- Visible rather than hidden.
- Easy to reject.
- Proportionate to the public goal.
- Based on evidence.
- Beneficial to the person or society.
- Subject to review.

A dark pattern does the opposite. It creates confusion, pressure, or unnecessary friction so that users choose what benefits the platform or institution.

## Cognitive warfare

Cognitive warfare refers to strategic attempts to influence perception, belief, emotion, judgment, and behavior. It may include:

- Disinformation.
- Psychological operations.
- Coordinated harassment.
- Identity-based polarization.
- Synthetic media.
- Information flooding.
- Attacks on trust in institutions.
- Attempts to create confusion rather than persuade a specific belief.

The phrase “AI hacks the brain” should be understood metaphorically. AI cannot directly rewrite human thought. It can, however, exploit the information environment surrounding human decision-making.

The objective may not always be to make citizens believe one particular falsehood. Sometimes the goal is to make them so confused or distrustful that they stop believing anyone.

## Digital government and democratic risk

Digital government can improve access to services and administrative efficiency. The 2024 UN survey found that 189 of 193 Member States offered at least one online public service. [desapublications.un](<https://desapublications.un.org/sites/default/files/publications/2024-10/Chapter%202%20%20E-Government%20Survey%202024.pdf>)

But digital government also creates risks involving:

- Centralized personal-data repositories.
- Biometric identification.
- Automated eligibility decisions.
- Predictive policing.
- Continuous surveillance.
- Data breaches.
- Exclusion of people without reliable internet access.
- Lack of appeal against automated decisions.

A digital system should never be judged only by whether it is fast. It must also be evaluated by whether citizens can understand it, challenge it, correct errors, and obtain a remedy.

A rights-respecting system should include data minimization, purpose limitation, security controls, human review, independent oversight, and appeal procedures.

## The “data dictatorship” idea

“Data dictatorship” is a warning term rather than a precise legal category. It describes a condition in which powerful institutions use data collection, surveillance, profiling, automated decisions, and information control with little accountability.

The risk becomes more serious when the same institution can:

1. Identify citizens.
2. Track their activities.
3. Infer their interests or vulnerabilities.
4. Predict their behavior.
5. Influence or punish them.
6. Prevent independent oversight.

The existence of digital government does not automatically create a dictatorship. The decisive questions concern legal limits, transparency, institutional checks, judicial review, citizen rights, and the ability to challenge abuse.

## AI agents and cybersecurity

AI agents can perform multi-step tasks, browse websites, call tools, write code, and interact with external systems. These capabilities can improve productivity and cybersecurity, but they also create new attack surfaces.

Important risks include:

- Prompt injection.
- Malicious instructions in webpages or documents.
- Unauthorized access to tools.
- Data leakage.
- Insecure browser automation.
- Excessive permissions.
- Incorrect interpretation of goals.
- Autonomous actions without human approval.

Secure agent design should use least-privilege access, sandboxing, audit logs, rate limits, human approval for high-impact actions, and clear rollback mechanisms.

An agent should not be allowed to transfer money, modify production systems, disclose confidential data, or make high-impact decisions merely because a natural-language instruction appears plausible.

## Protecting privacy and autonomy

Individuals cannot solve every structural problem alone, but personal practices reduce exposure:

- Use unique passwords and a password manager.
- Enable multi-factor authentication.
- Keep operating systems and applications updated.
- Limit unnecessary app permissions.
- Avoid publicly sharing information that enables impersonation.
- Treat urgent financial requests as unverified.
- Verify identity through an independent channel.
- Check original sources before sharing claims.
- Read laterally rather than trusting a single post.
- Be cautious with emotional, sensational, or unusually divisive content.
- Review advertising and privacy settings.
- Report fraud, impersonation, and manipulated media.

The most useful habit is to create a pause between receiving a message and acting on it.

## Institutional safeguards

Organizations should establish clear procedures for AI-related social-engineering risks.

### Technical safeguards

- Multi-factor authentication.
- Payment approval controls.
- Voice and identity verification.
- Content provenance.
- Synthetic-media labeling.
- Security testing.
- Monitoring and incident response.
- Data minimization.
- Access control and audit logging.

### Governance safeguards

- Independent audits.
- Algorithmic-impact assessments.
- Clear accountability.
- Public transparency reports.
- Political-ad disclosure.
- Researcher access under privacy safeguards.
- Human review of high-impact decisions.
- Citizen appeals and correction mechanisms.
- Protection for journalists, researchers, and whistleblowers.

NIST’s framework is useful because it does not treat risk assessment as a one-time checklist. Organizations should govern responsibilities, map system context and impacts, measure performance and harm, and manage risks throughout the system lifecycle. [airc.nist](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/playbook/)

## What should governments and platforms do?

Governments should establish clear rules for data protection, political advertising, synthetic media, identity fraud, automated decisions, and public-sector AI procurement.

Platforms should explain how recommender systems work at a meaningful level, label political and synthetic content where appropriate, provide user controls, publish transparency reports, and permit independent evaluation of systemic risks.

Neither government regulation nor platform self-regulation is sufficient by itself. Effective oversight requires cooperation among technical experts, policymakers, researchers, journalists, civil society, and affected communities.

## A balanced conclusion

AI is neither inherently benevolent nor inherently malicious. It is a set of tools whose effects depend on design, incentives, ownership, deployment, and governance.

The benefits of AI are substantial: better analysis, improved accessibility, scientific progress, more responsive services, and stronger defensive cybersecurity. The risks are also substantial: fraud, surveillance, synthetic media, privacy loss, manipulation, polarization, and weakened trust in democratic institutions.

The most accurate concern is not that AI will suddenly take complete control of human minds. The deeper concern is that institutions may gradually build opaque systems that influence what people see, believe, fear, purchase, share, and support—without giving them sufficient knowledge or control.

A democratic response should therefore focus on resilience rather than panic. Citizens need media literacy and privacy awareness. Organizations need secure procedures and accountable AI governance. Governments need enforceable rights, independent oversight, and transparent public systems. Platforms need to take responsibility for how recommendation and advertising systems affect public life.

The objective should not be to stop all influence. Influence is part of communication, education, and politics. The objective should be to ensure that influence is **transparent, contestable, proportionate, evidence-based, and compatible with human autonomy**.

AI should help people make better decisions—not quietly make decisions about people.
